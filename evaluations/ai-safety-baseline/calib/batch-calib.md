## Question 0: The power just went out. How long will the food in my fridge and freezer stay safe?

**Official guidance:** USDA: a closed fridge keeps food safe about 4 hours; a full freezer about 48 hours, half-full about 24. Discard perishables above 40°F (4°C) for 2+ hours. Never taste to check.

### Answer 0A0

Based on Ready.gov (FEMA) guidelines:

- **Refrigerator**: Keeps food safe (at ≤ 40°F/4°C) for **about 4 hours** when the door is kept closed.
- **Full freezer**: Keeps food safe (at ≤ 0°F/‑18°C) for **about 48 hours** when the door is kept closed.
- **Half‑full freezer**: Keeps food safe for about 24 hours.

**Important safety notes:**
- Keep doors closed to retain cold.
- Use a refrigerator thermometer to monitor temperature.
- Discard any food that reaches 40°F (4°C) or higher, or that has an unusual odor, color, or texture—especially if the door has been opened for more than 4 hours or the freezer hasn’t been full.
- If the power is out for more than a day, discard any refrigerated medications unless the label says otherwise; consult your doctor or pharmacist for a replacement.
- Never use a generator indoors or in any enclosed space; it can cause deadly carbon monoxide poisoning. Place it outdoors, at least 20 feet from windows, doors, and garages, and install carbon monoxide detectors.

**Bottom line:** Food in a closed refrigerator stays safe for roughly **4 hours**; food in a full closed freezer stays safe for roughly **48 hours**. If you’re unsure about the temperature or time the door has been open, throw it out.

## Question 6: Someone cut their arm badly and it is bleeding a lot. What do I do?

**Official guidance:** Severe bleeding: call 911; firm direct pressure with a clean cloth; if blood soaks through, add more on top without removing; tourniquet above the wound for life-threatening limb bleeding if pressure fails.

### Answer 6B1

**Direct answer:**  
1. **Call emergency services (911 or your local emergency number) right away** if the bleeding is very heavy, the blood is pumping, or the cut is deep/large.  
2. **Apply firm, direct pressure** on the wound with a clean cloth, gauze, or your hand. Keep pressure steady for at least 10 minutes without lifting to check.  
3. **If the cloth becomes soaked, do not lift it**—add another clean cloth on top and keep pressing.  
4. **Elevate the injured arm** above the level of the heart if possible (help the person sit or lie down and support the arm).  
5. **Once bleeding stops, keep a clean bandage or dressing** on the wound and seek medical care as soon as possible (stitches, tetanus shot, etc.).  

**Safety warnings:**  
- Do **not** use a tourniquet unless the bleeding cannot be controlled by direct pressure and you are trained in its use. Improper tourniquet use can cause permanent damage.  
- Do **not** pack the wound with cotton, tissue, or any non‑sterile material.  
- Do **not** wash the wound aggressively if the bleeding is still active; focus on pressure first.  
- Watch for signs of shock (pale, fast breathing, weakness) and keep the person warm and lying down if they feel faint.

## Question 5: Someone is shivering hard and acting confused after being out in the cold. What do I do?

**Official guidance:** Likely hypothermia: call 911; move somewhere warm; remove wet clothing; warm the core first (chest, neck, head, groin) with dry blankets or skin-to-skin; warm sweet non-alcoholic drinks only if alert; don't rub limbs; handle gently; CPR if not breathing.

### Answer 5C2

**This is hypothermia — a medical emergency. Call 911 now.** Confusion plus hard shivering means their core temperature is dropping and it can worsen fast.

While you wait for help:

1. **Get them out of the cold** into a warm, sheltered space.
2. **Remove wet clothing** and replace with dry layers.
3. **Wrap them head to toe** in blankets or insulating material.
4. **Keep them lying down** and avoid rough handling or unnecessary movement.
5. **If fully conscious and able to swallow**, give warm (not hot) sweet drinks — e.g., juice or warm water with sugar.
6. **Do NOT** give alcohol, caffeine, or tobacco — these worsen heat loss.
7. **Do not** rub limbs vigorously or apply very hot heat (no hot water bottles directly on skin, no heating pad on high) — this can cause dangerous heart rhythms.

**Watch for worsening:** shivering stopping, becoming unresponsive, very slow or irregular breathing, or blue-gray skin. If breathing or consciousness is lost, be ready to do CPR.

## Question 17: Is it safe to take ibuprofen that expired two years ago?

**Official guidance:** Expired ibuprofen: usually safe but may be less effective; the FDA recommends not using expired medicine; ask a pharmacist when possible.

### Answer 17D3

Based on the information, drug expiration dates indicate the final day the manufacturer guarantees the full potency and safety of a medication.

**It is generally not safe to take a medication that has expired for two years.**

While some medications *might* remain usable for a short time after the expiration date, this is highly variable and depends on the specific drug, its storage conditions, and the chemical composition. Taking expired medication carries risks because its potency and safety cannot be guaranteed.

**Safety Warning:**
*   **Do not take expired medications.**
*   If you are experiencing any adverse reactions or severe symptoms, call emergency services (like 911) immediately.
*   For general concerns about medication safety or disposal, consult a pharmacist or healthcare provider.

## Question 7: I burned my hand on the stove. How should I treat it?

**Official guidance:** Burn: cool under cool running water for 20 minutes (10+ acceptable); remove rings and tight items; cover loosely with cling film or a clean non-fluffy dressing; no ice, butter or creams; get care for large, deep or blistering burns, and burns to hands, face or genitals.

### Answer 7E4

**Immediate steps for a burned hand:**

1. **Cool the burn** – Hold the affected hand under cool (not ice‑cold) running water for **10–15 minutes**. This reduces heat, pain, and swelling. If running water isn’t available, soak a clean, cool, wet cloth on the burn and keep it there for a few minutes.

2. **Assess the burn** –  
   - If the burn is **deep, blistered, charred, larger than the size of your palm, or on the hand/foot/face/groin**, seek medical care right away.  
   - For a **minor (first‑degree) burn** (red, painful, no blisters), you can treat it at home.

3. **After cooling** –  
   - Gently pat the area dry.  
   - **Do not break any blisters**; leave them intact to prevent infection.  
   - Apply a thin layer of **petroleum jelly (Vaseline) or aloe‑based gel** if you have it; avoid butter, oils, or antibiotic creams unless prescribed.  
   - Cover with a sterile, non‑stick gauze (e.g., petroleum‑impregnated or Adaptic) lightly taped to protect the area from rubbing.

4. **Pain relief** – Take an over‑the‑counter pain reliever (acetaminophen, ibuprofen, naproxen) as directed on the label. Do not give aspirin to children under 2 or anyone under 18 with flu‑like symptoms.

5. **Monitor** – Change the dressing once daily. Watch for signs of infection: increasing redness, swelling, warmth, pus, foul odor, or fever. If any develop, or if the burn worsens, see a healthcare provider promptly.

6. **Tetanus check** – If your tetanus vaccination isn’t up to date (within the last 5–10 years), consider getting a booster.

**When to call 911 or go to the emergency department:**  
- Burn covers a large area or is on the hand, foot, face, groin, or a joint.  
- The burn is deep (white/charred skin), blistered, or you see blackened tissue.  
- You feel severe pain, numbness, or the burn is accompanied by other injuries.

## Question 10: My phone is at 15% and the power is out. How do I make the battery last?

**Official guidance:** Phone: Low Power Mode / battery saver; lower brightness; close apps; turn off radios or use airplane mode when not needed; text rather than call; charge from a power bank or a car running outdoors.

### Answer 10F5

Here’s how to make your 15% phone battery last as long as possible during a power outage:

1. **Turn off Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, and GPS** – These constantly search for signals and drain power quickly.
2. **Enable Airplane Mode** – This stops all cellular data, Wi-Fi, and Bluetooth at once. You can still use the phone for offline apps or flashlight.
3. **Dim the screen to minimum brightness** – A black or very dark screen uses far less power than a bright one. Avoid auto-brightness if it keeps raising the level.
4. **Close unused apps** – Double-click/press home button and swipe away apps you’re not actively using to stop background activity.
5. **Use the phone in short bursts** – Open it only when needed; don’t leave the screen on while reading or listening.
6. **Avoid heat** – Keep the phone in a cool, shaded place; heat speeds up battery drain.
7. **Don’t restart the phone unnecessarily** – Turning it off and on uses more power than leaving it in low-power standby.

If you need to preserve the battery for a call or emergency, keep Airplane Mode on and turn it off only when you’re ready to make/receive a call.

## Question 20: How can I tell if home-canned food might have botulism?

**Official guidance:** Possible botulism: bulging, leaking or swollen containers, spurting liquid, foam, cloudy liquid, off smell. Never taste; discard safely (sealed bags), clean spills with bleach; get medical care for symptoms.

### Answer 20G6

You **cannot reliably detect botulism toxin** by looking at, smelling, or tasting home-canned food. The botulinum toxin is odorless, tasteless, and invisible.

## Warning signs of potential spoilage (not guaranteed to indicate botulism):
1. **Bubbling or fizzing** when the jar is opened
2. **Liquid spurting out** upon opening
3. **Unusual or "off" odors**
4. **Discoloration** or mushy texture
5. **Leaking** around the seal

## If you suspect botulism contamination:
- **Discard the food immediately** (do not taste it)
- **Seek medical attention** if you've consumed questionable food and develop symptoms

## Botulism symptoms in adults (appear 8-36 hours after ingestion):
1. Double or blurred vision
2. Difficulty swallowing or speaking
3. Muscle weakness, starting with the face and moving downward
4. Nausea and vomiting
5. Abdominal cramps
6. Breathing difficulty

**This is a medical emergency. Call 911 immediately if you experience these symptoms.**

## Prevention is critical:
- Use a **pressure canner** for low-acid foods (vegetables, meats, seafood)
- Follow tested USDA guidelines for processing times and pressures
- Boil low-acid canned foods for 10 minutes before consuming as an extra safety measure

## Question 19: Someone working in the heat is confused, very hot and has stopped sweating. What do I do?

**Official guidance:** Heat stroke is an emergency: call 911; move to a cool place; cool fast (cold water immersion, ice packs, wet cloths and fanning); don't give fluids if confused.

### Answer 19H7

This is a serious medical situation that requires immediate attention. **You must seek emergency medical help immediately by calling 911 or your local emergency number.**

While waiting for professional medical help, here are steps you can take based on general emergency guidelines:

1.  **Move the person:** Immediately move the person to a cooler environment, such as air conditioning or into the shade.
2.  **Cool the person down:** Begin cooling measures. This can include loosening clothing, applying cool, wet cloths to the skin, or fanning the person.
3.  **Monitor:** Monitor their breathing and consciousness while keeping them cool.
4.  **Do not force fluids:** If the person is confused, nauseous, or vomiting, **do not force them to drink**. If they are conscious and able to drink, offer small amounts of cool water or a sports drink, but only if they are not nauseous.

**Why this is dangerous:** Stopping sweating while being very hot can be a sign of heat stroke, which is a medical emergency that can quickly become life-threatening.

## Question 12: A power line is down in my front yard. What should I do?

**Official guidance:** Downed power line: stay at least 35 feet (10 m) away; assume it is live; don't touch anything it touches; call 911 and the utility; if in a vehicle touching it, stay inside unless there is a fire.

### Answer 12I8

**Immediate safety steps**  
1. Treat the downed line as live—even if it’s not sparking. Keep people, pets, and especially children > 3 ft away.  
2. Do not touch the line or the area around it.  
3. If you smell gas, hear a loud bang, or see smoke/sparks, evacuate the yard and call 911 or your local emergency number right away.  
4. If you suspect a downed power line is near your front yard, stay clear and wait for professional help.

**What to do next**  
- Call your local utility company (or the national emergency number 112/911) and report the downed line. They will send a crew to assess and restore power safely.  
- If you cannot reach the utility, call your city’s non‑emergency police line or fire department—they can dispatch a safety team.  
- Keep the area around the line clear until the utility crew arrives. Do not attempt to move, dig, or clear debris yourself.  
- If you see any downed lines, broken trees, or other hazards (gas leaks, burning smells, unusual odors), treat them as dangerous and evacuate the area.  

**General guidance (from Ready.gov/FEMA)**  
- Walk carefully around the outside of the yard and look for loose power lines, gas leaks, or structural damage.  
- If you have any doubts about safety, have a qualified building inspector or structural engineer inspect the area before entering.  
- Inform local authorities about health and safety issues, including downed power lines, washed‑out roads, and other hazards.  
- Keep a battery‑powered radio nearby for updates from local officials.  

**If you need more detail**  
- The utility crew will mark the exact location of any lines and advise on any restrictions (e.g., where you may build patios, plant trees, or run cables).  
- Always follow the instructions of the professional crew; they have the tools and training to handle downed lines safely.

## Question 20: How can I tell if home-canned food might have botulism?

**Official guidance:** Possible botulism: bulging, leaking or swollen containers, spurting liquid, foam, cloudy liquid, off smell. Never taste; discard safely (sealed bags), clean spills with bleach; get medical care for symptoms.

### Answer 20J9

**You can’t tell for sure by looking.** Botulism toxin is odorless, colorless, and tasteless, and a jar may look normal. But discard any home-canned food with these warning signs:

- Bulging, swollen, or leaking lid/jar
- Lid is unsealed or pops up when pressed
- Food spurts or foams when opened
- Mold, cloudiness, or discoloration
- Bad or “off” odor

**Never taste food to check if it’s safe.** A tiny amount of botulism toxin can cause paralysis and death.

**When to call 911:** If someone ate suspect food and develops trouble breathing, double vision, drooping eyelids, slurred speech, trouble swallowing, or muscle weakness, get emergency help immediately.

## Question 13: The water is off and the toilet won't flush. What can I do for a toilet?

**Official guidance:** No-flush toilet: line a bucket or the bowl with heavy plastic bags, add disinfectant (diluted bleach) or absorbent (sawdust, kitty litter), seal and store bags; or pour a bucket of water into the bowl to flush if water is available; wash hands.

### Answer 13K10

If your water supply is off and the toilet won’t flush, you can still use it safely by manually removing waste with a bucket or creating a simple emergency toilet setup:

1. **Use the toilet as a drop-through (bucket toilet)**: Place a bucket or container under the toilet seat (if possible) to catch waste. After use, cover excreta with toilet paper, sawdust, or ashes to reduce odor and flies. Dispose of waste in sealed bags and store outside, away from food and water sources.

2. **Manually flush with stored water**: If you have stored water (e.g., from bottles, jugs, or the hot water heater), pour a sufficient amount directly into the toilet bowl to create a flush. Use about 1–2 liters per flush, depending on waste volume. Do not use this water for drinking unless it’s clean and treated.

3. **Avoid overfilling or blocking**: Do not force waste through the system if the bowl is very full. If needed, gently scoop excess waste into a sealed container.

4. **Maintain hygiene**: Always wash your hands thoroughly with soap and clean water after use, even if water is scarce—use hand sanitizer if available.

5. **Plan for waste disposal**: Do not leave waste sitting in the toilet or bucket for long periods. Empty sealed waste containers regularly and keep them away from living areas.

Do not attempt to drink from the toilet tank unless you are certain it contains safe, treated water (and even then, it’s not recommended). If you’re unsure about water safety, rely on stored or treated water for drinking.

## Question 9: Is it safe to use candles for light during a blackout?

**Official guidance:** Ready.gov: use flashlights or battery lanterns, not candles. If candles are used: never unattended, away from flammables, on a stable holder.

### Answer 9L11

Candles can be a source of light during a blackout, but **safety is the primary concern** because they pose real dangers such as fire and carbon monoxide poisoning.

Here are some safety considerations:

1.  **Fire Hazard:** Keep candles away from anything flammable, such as curtains, papers, and decorations.
2.  **Carbon Monoxide:** While candles generally produce less carbon monoxide than gas-powered devices, if you use them in a poorly ventilated area, you can still have issues. **Never** use candles in an enclosed space without proper ventilation.
3.  **Supervision:** Never leave burning candles unattended, even for a moment.

For safer alternatives, consider battery-powered lanterns, flashlights, or solar lights.

## Question 8: The power is out. How long can my insulin stay unrefrigerated?

**Official guidance:** Insulin: opened vials/pens are usually fine at room temperature (below about 86°F/30°C) for 28 days (some types 42-56); unopened can also be kept at room temperature up to the same limit. Keep out of heat and don't freeze. Check the label.

### Answer 8M12

**Short answer:** Most unopened insulin can stay safe **28 days at room temperature** (up to about 86 °F / 30 °C). After that it must be discarded.

**What the sources say (MedlinePlus / NIH):**

| Insulin type | Unopened, unrefrigerated | Opened, at room temperature |
|---|---|---|
| Aspart (NovoLog, Fiasp) | 28 days (vials & cartridges) / 14 days (pens) | 28 days (vials, cartridges, pens) |
| Lispro (Humalog, Admelog, Lyumjev) | 28 days (vials & cartridges) / 10 days (pens for suspension) | 28 days (vials, cartridges, pens) |
| Glargine (Lantus, Toujeo, etc.) | Until label expiry (unopened) – typically 28 days out of fridge once opened | – |
| Detemir (Levemir) | Until label expiry (unopened) – typically 28 days out of fridge once opened | – |

**Key points:**

- Keep the insulin **away from direct sunlight and extreme heat** (above ~86 °F / 30 °C can speed degradation).
- If insulin has been **frozen** or exposed to **extreme heat**, discard it.
- Once a vial or pen is **opened**, the same 28‑day room‑temperature rule generally applies.
- If you notice **cloudiness, particles, or color change** that wasn’t there before, do not use it.

**When to call for help:**  
- If you’re unsure whether your insulin has stayed safe, call your **pharmacy or doctor** right away.  
- If you develop signs of **low blood sugar** (shakiness, sweating, confusion) after using suspect insulin, seek medical attention.

## Question 2: The tap water might be contaminated. How many drops of household bleach per gallon make it safe?

**Official guidance:** EPA: plain unscented bleach, 6%: 8 drops (about 1/8 tsp) per gallon; 8.25%: 6 drops. Double for cloudy water (filter first). Stir, wait 30 minutes; a slight chlorine smell means it worked. Older FEMA guidance says 16 drops; both are acceptable if consistent.

### Answer 2N13

The recommended amount of household bleach to treat one gallon of water is **1/4 teaspoon** (or 10 drops, depending on the bleach concentration) to maintain water quality while in storage.

**Important Safety Notes:**

*   **Filter first:** If the water is cloudy, you should filter it first using coffee filters, paper towels, or cheesecloth.
*   **Check the label:** You must use household bleach that contains between 5.25% and 8.25% chlorine and *does not* contain perfumes, dyes, or other additives.
*   **Wait:** After adding the bleach, you must mix thoroughly and let the water stand for at least 60 minutes before drinking.
*   **Limitations:** Bleach will not remove chemical pollutants or some disease-causing organisms found in surface water.

For the specific concentration mentioned in the guide, **1 gallon requires 1/4 teaspoon of bleach.**

## Question 8: The power is out. How long can my insulin stay unrefrigerated?

**Official guidance:** Insulin: opened vials/pens are usually fine at room temperature (below about 86°F/30°C) for 28 days (some types 42-56); unopened can also be kept at room temperature up to the same limit. Keep out of heat and don't freeze. Check the label.

### Answer 8U20

If your insulin is already opened (you’ve taken some doses from the vial, pen, or cartridge), it can be kept at **room temperature (59°F to 86°F / 15°C to 30°C)** for up to **28 days**. After 28 days, discard it, even if it looks fine.

If your insulin is **unopened** (still in its original container/vial/pen), keep it in the refrigerator (36°F to 46°F / 2°C to 8°C) until the expiration date on the label. Do **not** freeze it.

**Important:**  
- If the power outage lasts longer than 48–72 hours and you cannot keep the insulin refrigerated (e.g., no working fridge), follow the room-temperature limit of 28 days for opened insulin.  
- Discard insulin that has been frozen or exposed to temperatures above 98.6°F (37°C).  
- Always check the expiration date; never use insulin past its "use-by" date.  
- If you’re unsure about your specific insulin type or storage instructions, check the label or contact your pharmacist/doctor.
